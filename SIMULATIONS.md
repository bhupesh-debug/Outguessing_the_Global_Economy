# Strategic Simulations — All Six, From the Research

Source: Part 18 v2, "Strategic Simulations" section, and the condensed
edition (Section 7). These are teaching models and scenario exercises,
not predictions. Think of each one as a chess position: the point is not
to guess the next move but to see the board from the other side — because
the other side is also looking at the board, and also trying to guess
your move. Probabilities, where given, are model judgements dated
November 6, 2026.

## Simulation A — The Fed Versus Global Investors

**Background.** Inflation above target, growth resilient (payrolls
+165,000), the Fed on hold at 3.75–4.00%, the market pricing a December
hike at about 76–78% after the payrolls surprise.

**Participants and incentives.** The Fed (credibility first — it cannot
afford to look soft on inflation after two dissents argued for a hike);
bond investors (positioned for a hike, vulnerable to a soft CPI); equity
investors (caught between good growth news and bad rate news); the dollar
(the release valve).

**Strategies.** The Fed can hike in December (validates the market, hurts
duration), hold (disappoints the hawkish repricing, helps equities,
weakens the dollar), or hike with dovish guidance (the compromise that
pleases nobody fully).

**Counterstrategies.** If the Fed hikes, investors rotate to
short-duration and cash; if it holds, the dollar falls and gold recovers;
if it hikes dovishly, the market hears the dovish half and rallies anyway
— which is why central banks fear being misunderstood more than being
wrong.

**Probability-weighted outcomes (model judgement, Nov 6, 2026):** hike
55%, hold 35%, hike-with-dovish-guidance 10%.

**Capital-flow consequence.** A hike keeps the dollar bid and
emerging-market pressure on; a hold releases it.

**Asset-price consequence.** The 10-year yield is the tell — above 5.00%
confirms the hawkish path; below 4.60% confirms the market overreacted.

## Simulation B — The United States Versus China

**Background.** Tariffs, trade restrictions, semiconductor competition,
supply-chain diversification, and a Chinese economy growing at 4.5% with
weak domestic demand and about 1.0% inflation.

**Participants and incentives.** Washington (leverage is market access and
technology), Beijing (leverage is manufacturing scale and rare-earth
processing), third countries (the beneficiaries of diversification —
India, Vietnam, Mexico — and the victims of being forced to choose).

**Strategies.** The US can escalate tariffs (hurts its own consumers,
pressures China), hold the line (freezes the status quo), or negotiate
sector by sector (slow, deniable, effective). China can retaliate
symmetrically (hurts its own exporters), devalue quietly (helps
exporters, alarms the US), or stimulate domestically (slow, expensive,
but the only durable answer).

**Counterstrategies.** Each side's retaliation invites the other's
diversification — which is why the most likely outcome is neither a deal
nor a war but a slow, grinding decoupling, with India and Southeast Asia
as the swing destinations for capital.

**Winners:** diversified manufacturing hubs, gold (as a hedge on
escalation). **Losers:** single-market exporters, tariff-exposed supply
chains.

## Simulation C — India Versus Global Capital

**Background.** Foreign investors sold about $31 billion of Indian
equities in 2026, domestic institutions bought about $75 billion, the
rupee sits near 94.5, the RBI has just hiked to 5.50%, and yet the Nifty
surged 2.01% on November 6.

**The question.** Can India outperform even when foreign investors are
selling? The data says it already has — the market rose while foreigners
sold, because the domestic bid is larger than the foreign exit.

**Participants and incentives.** FPIs (return-sensitive, dollar-sensitive,
valuation-sensitive); DIIs and SIP flows (sticky, rupee-denominated,
valuation-insensitive); the RBI (caught between defending the rupee and
supporting growth).

**Strategies.** FPIs can return at scale (if US yields fall), trickle back
(if yields plateau), or stay away (if the rupee keeps sliding). The RBI
can hike again (supports the rupee, hurts growth), intervene in FX (burns
reserves, buys time), or let the rupee slide (helps exporters, imports
inflation). The domestic bid can hold the market regardless — and in 2026
it has.

**Probability-weighted outcomes (model judgement):** FPI trickle-back
50%, return at scale 30%, continued exit 20%.

**The strategic insight.** The marginal buyer has changed nationality,
and models that only watch FPI flows are watching the wrong crowd.

## Simulation D — OPEC and Global Oil

**Background.** Brent near $98 a barrel, with the IMF's forecast assuming
about $96 for 2026 and a reopening of the Strait of Hormuz by March 2027
— two assumptions that carry the entire global growth forecast.

**Participants and incentives.** OPEC+ producers (revenue vs market
share); the US and Iran (the standoff that moved prices above $100
earlier in the autumn); India and China (the importers who pay for every
dollar of Brent).

**Strategies.** Producers can restrain supply (higher prices, lower
volumes), flood the market (lower prices, market share), or use the threat
of disruption as leverage. Importers can build strategic reserves,
diversify suppliers, or pass the cost through to inflation and let the
central bank deal with it.

**Capital-flow and asset-price consequence.** For India the chain is
brutally simple: Brent above $110 means a wider current account, a weaker
rupee, higher inflation, and a higher repo rate — which is why the oil
price is the single most important external variable in the India engine,
and why the IMF's Hormuz assumption is flagged as an assumption, not a
fact.

## Simulation E — Central-Bank Currency Strategy

**Background.** The dollar index at 102.32, up from a 52-week low of
95.55; gold off a $5,318 record; central banks buying more gold
year-to-date than at the same point in 2025.

**Participants and incentives.** The Fed (the dollar is its by-product,
not its target); the RBI (intervening near 94.35, according to dealer
reports); the PBOC (managing the yuan's slide); reserve managers
everywhere (diversifying slowly, because quickly would move the market
against them).

**Strategies.** Intervene (buys time, burns reserves), hike (supports the
currency, hurts growth), accumulate gold (diversifies without announcing
it), or let the currency slide (exports the problem).

**The game-theory point.** Reserve diversification is a classic
crowded-exit problem: if everyone diversifies at once, the dollar falls
and gold spikes — which is why everyone diversifies slowly, and why the
slow, steady central-bank gold bid is more informative than any single
week's price. Gold's 2026 story — a record high in January, a nine-month
correction, a rebound off the October 30 low of $3,885.70 — is the price
of that slow game being played in public.

## Simulation F — Institutional Investor Competition

**Background.** The same payrolls report, the same Fed hold, the same
Nifty surge — and six different investors doing six different things.

**Participants and incentives.** Pension funds rebalance on calendar, not
on news, and buy what has fallen. Hedge funds trade the surprise itself,
in and out in hours. Banks manage balance sheets and regulatory capital,
not views. Insurers buy duration to match liabilities. Retail investors
chase the story they just read. Index funds buy everything, in
proportion, whatever happens.

**The lesson.** There is no "the market" — there are many markets, layered
on top of each other, and the price is where their orders cross. The
strategist's edge is knowing which layer is marginal today: on November 6,
the marginal layer in India was the domestic institution and the returning
foreigner; in the US, it was the fast-money fund trading the payrolls
surprise. Tomorrow the marginal layer may be different, and the price will
be different with it.
